## Why old CS2 reviews remain visible

Search indexes do not work like a live inventory system. A review can keep its backlinks, ranking signals, and historical clicks even when the checkout dies. A screenshot can look fresh because the design aged well. An article title may say “updated” while the product evidence inside it is years old.

Affiliate content has another lag: the page may continue earning traffic after its commercial target disappears. Even an honest writer may not notice that a status page changed. That is why a search-result position is evidence of discoverability, not current availability.

Historical reviews still have uses. They can document former features, positioning, and market context. They become misleading when they use present tense, repeat undated protection claims, or send readers to a dead purchase path.

The first third of any closure-led alternatives article should therefore resolve status before discussing replacements. Otherwise the page exploits the old brand query without answering the reader’s real question.

## What counts as an active replacement

For this article, “active when checked” requires four independent signals:

1. **Reachable official product page.** A homepage alone is weaker than a game-specific page.
2. **Current access path.** A working download, trial, account, or purchase route must be visible.
3. **Dated update evidence.** A changelog, release, status notice, or recent official activity should show maintenance.
4. **Visible support route.** The user should be able to identify where current access questions go.

A candidate that meets only one signal is not automatically dead, but it is not strong enough for a confident active recommendation. A provider can also be reachable while its product is paused. Verify the game-specific state, not just the domain.

## The six-signal stale review detector

Run this detector on every old “best CS2” article before trusting its verdict.

### 1. Old screenshots

The UI, domain, prices, or game branding no longer match the official page. A screenshot without a date is weak evidence.

### 2. Dead checkout

The buy button loops, errors, redirects to an unrelated domain, or never reaches a current offer. Do not treat a visible button as a working access path.

### 3. Missing status page

The review quotes availability but does not link a status, changelog, release, or official announcement. Evergreen marketing copy is not incident history.

### 4. Last update absent

There is no dated maintenance signal for the game. “Regularly updated” is only a provider claim when no date is attached.

### 5. Broken support link

The Discord, Telegram, email, ticket form, or forum route is gone or no longer official. Do not replace a broken official route with an unverified community contact.

### 6. Undated safety claims

The review repeats “safe” or “undetected” without date, method, scope, or attribution. Those words cannot prove a current or future account outcome.

Two signals are enough to stop and research again. Four or more usually mean the article is historical even if it still ranks.

## How to check status before paying or downloading

Do the public checks in this order:

1. Open the exact game page and record the final URL and time.
2. Confirm that the access action leads somewhere current, but do not complete it just to test a review.
3. Find the latest dated CS2 maintenance evidence.
4. Check whether support is official, reachable, and consistent with the website.
5. Compare the product page with the homepage, documentation, and checkout for contradictory access terms.
6. Save screenshots for any claim that may change.

This process is intentionally boring. That is a feature. Closure research is about eliminating stale facts, not manufacturing excitement.
