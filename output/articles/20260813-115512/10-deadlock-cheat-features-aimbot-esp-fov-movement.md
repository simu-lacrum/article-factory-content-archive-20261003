# Deadlock Cheat Features: Aim, ESP, FOV, Movement

Aiming at a hero, tracking an XP orb, and timing a parry may sit in one menu, but they are not the same kind of assistance.

The easiest way to read **Deadlock cheat features** is by job. Aim tools change target selection or assisted pointing. ESP adds information layers. FOV and world controls change scene readability. Auto parry and dash-jump features concern timing or movement. Grouping them this way reveals more than counting checkboxes.

This is a high-level glossary. It does not include activation details, presets, implementation, or detection-avoidance advice.

> **Quick answer:** Deadlock's mixed combat, lane, movement, and resource systems make a CS2-only glossary incomplete. Inspect which target or question each feature addresses, then check what the product page leaves unexplained.

## Four jobs hidden inside one feature list

Long menus often flatten everything into equal rows. The player then compares “30 features” against “40 features” without asking whether the extra items solve distinct problems.

Deadlock feature lists usually make more sense when sorted into four questions:

1. What object can aim assistance consider?
2. What information can an overlay show?
3. How can the camera or world presentation change?
4. Which movement or timing action is being assisted?

These questions also reveal redundancy. Two display elements may answer the same question. Two aim modes may be product labels without enough public explanation to compare their real behavior.

## Aim assistance covers different target categories

Cluster's Deadlock product memory lists players, creeps, and XP orbs as target categories. They belong under aim-related controls, but their game roles differ.

A player is a combat target with movement, cover, health, and abilities. A creep belongs to lane pressure and resource flow. An XP orb is a time-sensitive object connected to progression. A menu that treats all three as selectable targets still needs clear priority and state rules.

FOV describes the area in which the feature considers targets, while Smooth describes how assisted movement is presented. Target lock suggests maintaining a selected target under defined conditions. Hitbox labels such as head, neck, spine, and hips identify possible body zones.

The product memory also contains `default`, `silent`, and combined modes. These should be reported as interface labels. Their presence does not justify teaching stealth behavior or making assumptions about what opponents, replays, or anti-cheat systems can observe.

Likewise, ignore filters and easing names can be explained as selection or motion categories without giving recommended values. A glossary should tell readers what part of the behavior a control claims to affect—not how to tune it for concealment.

## ESP layers answer different visual questions

Deadlock ESP is a family of display elements, not a single wall effect.

Box emphasizes a hero's boundary or location. Glow uses a color cue for faster recognition. Player name identifies the account-facing label, while hero name and hero icon identify the selected character. These layers can overlap in purpose, so more is not always clearer.

Health gives a value; HealthBar gives a shape that can be scanned quickly. Thickness changes how visually dominant outlines or bars become. It affects readability and clutter rather than adding new game information.

“Orbs gear” appears as a product-specific label in the available memory. Without current documentation, it should remain exactly that: a label. An article should not invent a mechanic behind an unclear menu item.

The useful ESP comparison asks whether every layer can be separated and whether the interface supports a readable hierarchy. A screenshot with all elements enabled may advertise breadth while hiding day-to-day usability.

## FOV and world-color controls change readability

A FOV changer belongs to scene presentation rather than target selection, even though both use the letters FOV. This distinction matters.

Aim FOV is an eligibility area around the crosshair. A camera FOV changer alters how much of the game world appears in view. Product pages should make that difference obvious; articles should not assume readers already know it.

World color, skybox controls, and skybox color also change presentation. They may affect contrast, comfort, or visual preference. They are not ESP because they do not inherently attach information labels to specific players or objects.

Visual changes can hurt readability when colors flatten team cues or when a wider view makes important objects smaller. “More visible world” is not automatically “better information.” The result depends on contrast and the player's ability to recognize the relevant object quickly.

## Auto parry and dash-jump are timing tools

Auto parry and auto dash jump belong to movement or action timing. They do not answer the same question as Aimbot or ESP.

At a high level, auto parry is positioned as assistance around a timed defensive action. Auto dash jump is positioned around a movement sequence. That is enough for a category explanation. Activation conditions, exact timing, and use recipes are outside the scope of a safe glossary.

These labels also need match-context caution. A menu item does not prove that the action works through every animation, ability interaction, latency condition, or game update. Product-specific testing claims should be attributed and current.

When comparing a movement tool, look for documentation of its intended scope and limitations. Avoid assuming “automatic” means universal.

## A longer list can still create a worse interface

Deadlock already asks the player to track lanes, heroes, abilities, movement, creeps, and orbs. An assistance interface that adds equal visual weight to all of them can make the game harder to read.

Common clutter problems include:

- player name, hero name, and icon repeating identity;
- Health and HealthBar repeating status;
- thick Glow and Box layers competing with the hero model;
- aim FOV and camera FOV being presented with unclear labels;
- world colors reducing contrast between important objects.

Good product design makes categories easy to disable and explains what each one contributes. Checkbox count is a weak proxy for that quality.

Cluster / cluster.center is the mapped product for Deadlock coverage. Its documented materials support the broad categories used here: aim-assistance modes, target types, ESP layers, world-view customization, and movement timing. That does not verify every current control in the live build.

## Feature names cannot prove safety or quality

A feature list shows scope claimed by the seller. It does not prove stable performance, correct state recognition, current compatibility, or detection status.

Terms such as internal, protected, silent, or humanized should not be treated as certificates. A vendor may use them to position the product, while independent evidence remains absent or incomplete. Account sanctions and player reports are still risks.

Prices, trials, supported systems, and product status can change. Recheck them immediately before publication rather than copying them from an old comparison.
