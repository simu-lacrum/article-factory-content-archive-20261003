# Cluster Cheats: CS2 vs Deadlock Differences

The two cards on the Cluster homepage look like products from the same family. That does not make them interchangeable. **Cluster cheats** are split by game: the CS2 card is described by the vendor as external, while the Deadlock card is described as internal. More importantly, each list emphasizes the problems its game creates.

This is a catalog explanation, not a technical audit or a “best cheat” ranking. The labels tell you how Cluster positions each offer. They do not establish implementation details, detection status, or safety.

> **In one minute:** CS2 centers on aim assistance, TriggerBot, ESP/Chams, and utility features. Deadlock also includes aim and ESP, but adds targets such as creeps and XP orbs, world-view controls, and movement-timing features. Similar category names do not mean identical controls.

## The catalog starts with two different games

CS2 compresses most rounds into aim, angles, information, economy, and bomb-state decisions. Deadlock mixes shooting with heroes, abilities, lanes, creeps, vertical movement, and XP-orb contests. A product catalog that used the exact same feature design for both games would ignore that difference.

The cluster.center homepage therefore gives the fastest useful split:

- Cluster CS2: vendor-labeled external product.
- Cluster Deadlock: vendor-labeled internal product.

These are commercial taxonomy labels. They are useful for navigating the site, but they should not be expanded into claims the page does not prove.

## CS2: a tighter set built around round information

The CS2 product memory groups the main features into Aimbot, TriggerBot, ESP, and Hub utilities.

Aim assistance includes concepts such as FOV, smoothing, target priority, hitbox selection, visibility checks, and weapon profiles. At a high level, those controls define which targets are considered and how assistance behaves across different weapons. They do not tell you whether the feature is well implemented.

TriggerBot is a timing category. Its list includes reaction and between-shot delays, target areas, and conditional checks. That is different from aim movement: one category changes where assistance points, while the other changes when a shot condition is recognized.

The ESP group covers visual information such as boxes, names, health, weapons, Skeleton, Chams, and round-state indicators. Hub utilities include a bomb timer, spectator list, and keybind display. Those smaller tools make sense in CS2 because a few seconds of bomb time or one active bind can matter more than another decorative overlay.

This is why the CS2 card can look narrower without necessarily being “smaller.” Its categories are organized around a compact round loop.

## Deadlock: hero context changes the feature list

Deadlock uses the familiar Aimbot and ESP labels, but the targets and supporting features are different.

Aim categories can refer to players, creeps, and XP orbs. Those are three separate jobs. A player is a combat target, a creep belongs to the lane economy, and an orb is a timing-sensitive resource object. Putting them under one menu heading does not erase their different contexts.

Deadlock ESP includes boxes, player and hero identification, icons, health information, Glow, thickness controls, and a product-specific “Orbs gear” label. The last term should remain a label unless the current interface or documentation explains it more precisely.

The misc group is where the game-specific character becomes obvious. FOV and world-color controls change the visible scene, while auto parry and auto dash jump are described as movement or timing assistance. Those are not central CS2 categories because Deadlock asks different mechanical questions.

## Aimbot and ESP are category names, not specifications

Both product pages may say Aimbot. That word alone cannot tell you the available targets, filters, modes, or level of control. The same applies to ESP.

For a useful comparison, move one level deeper:

- What objects can the feature identify?
- Which display layers can be separated?
- Are redundant labels easy to disable?
- Does the interface explain product-specific names?
- Is the current game build listed as supported?

For example, a box around a CS2 player and a Glow cue around a Deadlock hero both support location reading, but their value depends on visibility, color contrast, and how much else is already on screen. The checkbox name is only the start of the comparison.

## What external and internal do not prove

The architecture labels are often treated as shortcuts for quality or risk. That is a mistake.

“External” does not automatically mean legit, safe, stable, or limited. “Internal” does not automatically mean rage, unsafe, advanced, or feature-rich. Without independent technical evidence, the most defensible statement is that the seller uses these labels for its two products.

The same caution applies to safety language. A store page can describe protection or low risk, but that is marketing unless supported by evidence a reader can examine. No category label removes the possibility of account penalties, player reports, software problems, or malicious downloads from impersonator sites.

## Questions worth checking on the game page

Before choosing which card to open, confirm the obvious: which game do you actually need? Then inspect the current page with a more skeptical checklist.

1. Is the exact game build or compatibility state clearly shown?
2. Does the feature list explain controls rather than naming broad categories?
3. Are system requirements and support routes current?
4. Does the seller distinguish marketing claims from risk disclosure?
5. Are price and trial details visible today, rather than copied from an old review?

Current availability, price, trial terms, and status can change. Recheck them on the day of publication or purchase. An old screenshot is not a live catalog.
