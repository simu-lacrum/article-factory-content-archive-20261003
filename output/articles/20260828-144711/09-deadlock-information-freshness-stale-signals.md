# Deadlock Information Freshness: When an Old Signal Is Worse Than No Signal

Deadlock information freshness matters because the map keeps moving after every signal. A last-seen position can help predict a route, protect a lane, or warn about a roof. The same marker becomes dangerous when it looks current after the player has had time to change level, catch a zipline, or join another fight.

No signal creates uncertainty, which usually makes a player check. A stale signal can remove that healthy doubt. It offers a precise location with expired authority, and precision is persuasive even when it describes the past.

## Every signal needs a timestamp, even if it has no clock

A player should be able to distinguish three states at a glance:

- Confirmed now: direct sight, a fresh event, or current teammate contact.
- Recent: useful for predicting the next reachable area.
- Historical: evidence that shapes caution but no longer names a position.

The interface does not need a number beside every marker. Opacity, saturation, shape, or a short decay can communicate age faster. What matters is that an old marker cannot be mistaken for a live one.

That visual rule must remain consistent. If dim sometimes means “far away” and sometimes means “old,” the player has to decode the symbol under pressure. Give freshness its own stable channel.

## The map determines how fast information decays

Signal age is not only a stopwatch problem. It depends on what the subject could have done since the event.

A last-seen point in a closed lane with one exit may stay useful longer. A point beside a roof transition, bridge, corner, or zipline branches into several plausible positions almost immediately. The more reachable choices exist, the faster location certainty should collapse.

Use a reachability question: “Where could this player be now?” If the answer is still one narrow area, the old signal has tactical value. If the answer is three levels and two routes, the marker should behave like a warning about possibilities, not a claim about location.

## A marker can remain useful after it stops being literal

Old information does not need to disappear from thought. It needs a new role.

Suppose an opponent was last seen leaving a lane toward an elevated route. Seconds later, the exact point is stale. Yet the event still suggests that the original lane may have less pressure and the upper approach deserves a check. The information moved from position to hypothesis.

This distinction prevents two bad extremes: trusting every marker until contradicted, or discarding all history the instant direct sight ends. Good decisions carry history with decreasing confidence.

In a review, describe that change explicitly: “Confirmed on lane,” then “recent toward bridge,” then “unconfirmed in upper network.” Language that reflects uncertainty creates better calls and better interface criteria.

## Stale certainty causes predictable decision errors

An old signal is worse than none when it suppresses a necessary check. Common errors include:

- Committing to a last-seen point while the target reaches a side route.
- Leaving a lane unguarded because a marker still suggests the opponent is elsewhere.
- Following a historical path across levels as if it were continuous tracking.
- Ignoring fresh teammate contact because the older marker is visually louder.
- Spending an escape or movement resource on a route that is no longer relevant.

Notice that the problem is not merely inaccuracy. It is confidence. A softly fading hint invites validation. A hard, bright marker invites action.

## Add uncertainty to the visual hierarchy

Priority and confidence are separate. A high-impact opponent may deserve attention even when their exact position is uncertain. A low-impact event may be precisely located but irrelevant to the next fight.

Build the hierarchy from four questions:

1. How recent is the signal?
2. How many routes were reachable afterward?
3. Can the subject affect the current fight now?
4. What newer evidence confirms or contradicts it?

This produces more honest displays. A recent nearby threat can be strong and specific. An important but missing opponent can be prominent yet deliberately imprecise. An old low-impact event can fade out.

Do not solve uncertainty with more tiny text. The relationship should be visible before it is read.

## Know when to pause before acting

Fast games reward quick action, but a half-second validation can save a full commitment. Pause when the signal sits near several route branches, when it is older than the likely travel time to your flank, or when fresh sound and teammate information disagree with it.

The pause does not mean standing still in the open. It can mean holding a safe edge, checking the minimap, asking for confirmation, or choosing a reversible movement instead of spending the full route.

A useful rule is: the more expensive the action, the fresher the evidence should be. Turning the camera is cheap. Leaving cover, crossing levels, or abandoning an objective is expensive. Old information may justify the first and fail to justify the second.

## Test freshness in sequences, not screenshots

A screenshot can show color and clutter, but not decay. Record a short sequence with one event, then watch how the signal changes while the subject could move through the map.

Test three conditions:

- One exit: does the signal remain useful without overstating certainty?
- Several exits: does it soften fast enough?
- New contradiction: does fresh information take visual priority immediately?

Then repeat during a crowded team fight. An elegant fade in isolation may disappear too quickly amid effects, while a readable marker may linger too loudly once several events stack.

## A freshness checklist for live review

1. Identify the last confirmed event.
2. Note its age and the routes reachable since then.
3. Separate exact position from likely area.
4. Check for newer confirming or contradictory evidence.
5. Match the confidence of the signal to the cost of the action.
6. Let the signal fade when it can no longer support a decision.

Deadlock information freshness is not about deleting history. It is about changing how strongly history speaks. A good signal starts specific, becomes a hypothesis, and eventually yields to uncertainty. When that decay is visible, the player can act quickly without pretending the map stopped moving.

## FAQ

### How old is too old for a last-seen marker?

There is no universal number. It depends on movement speed, route branches, vertical transitions, and the cost of the decision it supports.

### Should old signals disappear completely?

Not always. They may remain useful as low-confidence route context, provided they cannot be mistaken for current position.

### What should happen when new evidence contradicts a marker?

The fresh evidence should take priority immediately, and the old marker should weaken or change role.

### Can marker freshness indicate product safety?

No. Information design says nothing about account risk or current support. Verify changing product claims independently and avoid guarantees.
