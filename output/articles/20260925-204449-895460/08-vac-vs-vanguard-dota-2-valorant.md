# VAC vs Vanguard: What Dota 2 and VALORANT Publish

VAC and Riot Vanguard are different anti-cheat products, but a difference in architecture does not prove that a third-party tool is safe. The useful comparison stays inside public documentation and labels everything else unknown.

> **Quick answer:** compare the stated purpose, where the client component runs, how updates are described and what each company says about enforcement. Do not infer a private tool’s detection status from a marketing slogan or from the absence of an immediate ban.

## Start with categories, not headlines

When people ask “VAC vs Vanguard,” they often mix four questions:

- What software component is installed?
- When does it run?
- What data or signals does the provider publicly discuss?
- How and when can enforcement happen?

Keep the questions separate. A public description of a driver, service or server check is not a complete map of an anti-cheat system.

## What public VAC material establishes

Steam Support describes VAC as Valve Anti-Cheat, a system used across Steam games. The documentation explains the policy boundary around cheating and VAC bans, but it does not publish every internal signal, review rule or enforcement timetable for Dota 2.

That means a useful article can say what Valve documents and stop there. It should not invent a live “detected list,” a ban-wave calendar or a guaranteed delay.

## What Riot publishes about Vanguard

Riot describes Vanguard as the anti-cheat platform used for VALORANT, with a client and a kernel-mode driver component. Riot also publishes support material about installation, operation and troubleshooting. Those pages explain the supported product context; they do not provide a universal method for predicting every enforcement decision.

The systems are therefore not interchangeable labels. Their public explanations, deployment choices and support paths differ. That does not make one a loophole for third-party software.

## Privacy and maintenance are separate questions

A player can reasonably ask what starts with Windows, what can be uninstalled and how updates are communicated. Those are maintenance and privacy questions. They are not the same as “will a cheat be detected?”

For each system, record:

- the official component description;
- the documented support channel;
- update and troubleshooting notes;
- the platform or game scope;
- the facts that remain unpublished.

Use the same fields for both products. Symmetry keeps the comparison from becoming a disguised verdict.

## What the comparison cannot tell you

Public documentation cannot establish the current internal rule set, every signal sent by the client or the exact timing of an enforcement action. A third-party tool launching without an immediate penalty proves only that no visible penalty happened in that window.

Do not use VAC vs Vanguard as a shortcut for account-risk advice. Read the platform policy, then make a separate decision about whether an unofficial tool belongs on the account you care about.

## FAQ

### Is VAC the same as Vanguard?

No. They are different anti-cheat products with different public documentation and deployment contexts.

### Does Vanguard prove that Dota 2 is less safe for cheats?

No. Product architecture differences do not establish a third-party tool’s current detection status or account outcome.

### Does an immediate ban delay prove safety?

No. It only shows that no visible penalty occurred during that observation window.

### Where should I check current policy?

Use official Steam Support, Riot Support and game updates. Treat third-party claims as marketing unless independently supported.

### Can CheatsGaming promise a zero-ban result?

No. Neither a publisher nor a product page should turn unknown enforcement behavior into a guarantee.
