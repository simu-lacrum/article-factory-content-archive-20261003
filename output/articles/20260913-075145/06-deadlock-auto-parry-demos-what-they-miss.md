# Deadlock Auto-Parry Demos: What They Miss

Deadlock Auto-Parry demos are good at showing the clean moment: an opponent commits to melee, the reaction fires, and the result looks effortless. What they often miss is the context that determines whether the feature is understandable, current, and useful outside a hand-picked clip.

A ten-second GIF can demonstrate that a behavior existed. It cannot tell you how the system handles feints, crowded fights, changing game conditions, or a hero interaction that was not included in the recording.

## A demo shows a moment, not a coverage map

The first question is not “Did it parry?” The better question is “What situation was actually tested?”

Look for the missing context:

- Was the clip captured in a controlled test or a real match?
- Was only one opponent present?
- Did the attacker repeat the same obvious pattern?
- Is the game build or capture date visible?
- Are failed reactions included anywhere?

Most marketing clips optimize for clarity. That is reasonable, but clarity can become selection bias when only perfect outcomes survive editing.

## Feints expose the difference between reaction and judgment

Melee interactions are not purely mechanical. Players threaten, delay, cancel, and reposition. A feature that reacts to a clear commitment may look excellent in isolation and behave very differently when the opponent is actively trying to draw it out.

This does not mean a demo is useless. It means the viewer should separate two jobs:

1. recognizing an incoming event;
2. deciding whether responding now is correct.

Automation can make the first job look consistent. The second depends on match context. A video that never shows uncertainty cannot explain how the product handles it.

## Crowded fights are the real readability test

One-versus-one footage is easy to parse. Real Deadlock fights can include overlapping heroes, movement, effects, terrain, and multiple threats. A meaningful demo set should include more than the clean lane scenario.

Ask whether the product explains target priority, irrelevant triggers, and what happens when several melee events occur close together. You do not need low-level implementation details. You do need a clear behavioral description.

The same applies to feedback. Can the user tell when the module is active, waiting, or unavailable? A silent checkbox gives less confidence than a status label and documented limitation.

## What a complete evaluation should include

Use a small evidence ladder:

- **Level one:** a labeled screenshot or short GIF;
- **Level two:** a dated demonstration with visible context;
- **Level three:** documented behavior and known exclusions;
