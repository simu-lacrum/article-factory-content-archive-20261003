## The real problem is equal-weight information

Clutter is not simply “too much stuff.” It is a hierarchy failure: a distant event, a nearby threat, and a resource marker all demand the same attention. When every layer is bright and persistent, the player has to rebuild priority from scratch during every fight. The fix is to rank signals by the question they answer, then lower or remove anything that cannot affect the current decision.

## Priority one: immediate survival

Start with what can end your participation right now: current health, the closest credible threat, available cover or exit, and a short defensive window. These signals should be easiest to locate and hardest to confuse with background context. Distant objectives can wait for a heartbeat. If a visual element does not help you survive, disengage, or commit during the next moment, it cannot occupy the top layer.

## Priority two: the next actionable target

Once survival is readable, surface the opponent or objective you can actually affect next. Target identity, proximity, and immediate availability matter more than a wall of distant possibilities. The landing page for [Deadlock cheats](https://melonity.gg/en/deadlock) groups visuals, aim assistance, parry, and item-related categories, which is a useful example of why category names still need a priority order. The same audit applies when reviewing a cluster.center profile.

## Priority three: souls, power-ups, and map activity

Souls, power-ups, allies, and map events become important when they change the next route or timing window. They belong in a quieter layer until that happens. A soul near the current contest can be actionable; one far across the map is context. A power-up matters when a rotation is plausible, not merely because it exists. Good triage promotes background information only when distance, timing, and intention make it relevant.

## Automation must remain legible

Any automated category should make its boundary obvious. The user needs to know whether it is active, what broad condition it responds to, and when manual judgment takes over. Parry- or item-related categories become harder to trust when their behavior is hidden behind an all-purpose switch. Legibility is the standard: visible state, predictable scope, and a clean off position. Complexity that cannot be explained briefly should not run silently in the background.

## Reduce clutter with a one-question rule

Assign one question to every element: “Am I in danger?”, “What can I act on?”, or “What changes my route?” If two elements answer the same question, keep the clearer one. If an element answers nothing during the current phase, remove or mute it. Review the screen at normal speed, not while paused. The goal is not a pretty screenshot; it is a faster decision when six players and several objectives compete for attention.

## A 30-second pre-match audit

- Can I locate immediate danger at a glance?
- Is the next actionable target distinct from background markers?
- Are distant resources visually quieter?
- Does every active automation category have a clear state?
- Are duplicate signals removed?
- Can I explain each remaining element in one sentence?

## Conclusion

Information wins attention only when it earns urgency. Build the hierarchy from survival outward, promote context when it becomes actionable, and keep every automated category visible enough to understand and stop.

## FAQ

### Is more visual information always better in Deadlock?

No. Extra information can slow decisions when every signal has equal weight.

### Which information should be most prominent during a fight?

Immediate survival, followed by the next actionable threat or objective.

### How often should a visual profile be reviewed?

Review it after meaningful changes and whenever repeated signals stop affecting decisions.

### Can automation replace situational awareness?

No. It can organize inputs, but the player still owns context and judgment.
