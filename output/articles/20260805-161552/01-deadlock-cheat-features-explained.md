## Why Deadlock feature lists are unusually confusing

Deadlock mixes projectile shooting with lanes, Souls, movement and MOBA-style ability timing. That creates overlapping labels: “aim” might refer to enemy heroes, creeps or floating Souls, while “visuals” might mean combat information or broad world presentation. Long lists flatten those differences. Read each category by asking what it observes, when that information matters, how clearly it communicates, and how much control stays with the player.

## Aim assistance: heroes, creeps and Souls are different jobs

Deadlock aim features should be separated by target and purpose. Hero assistance concerns moving opponents during combat, where projectile travel and target selection matter. Creep assistance belongs to lane pressure and routine farming decisions. Soul assistance deals with a separate, brief contest after units die. A product may group all three under “aim,” yet support only some target types or treat them differently. The label therefore says little by itself; useful descriptions identify the target class, the decision being assisted, and whether the player can understand and interrupt the behavior.

## Visual awareness: information must answer a decision

Deadlock visual awareness can include enemy boxes, names, hero identity, health, bars, glow, map activity, cooldown context, FOV changes or world-color adjustments. Quantity is a weak measure. A visual layer earns its place when it answers a decision—who is exposed, who is low, or where attention should move—without burying the match in noise. Readability, hierarchy and the ability to disable irrelevant layers matter more than a giant checklist. ## Lane economy: why Souls deserve their own category

Deadlock Souls assistance belongs under lane economy, not hero combat. Souls appear in a short resource contest that directly affects income and denial, so the relevant question is whether a feature addresses that economy event at all—not whether it can track an enemy hero. Keeping Souls separate prevents a broad “aim” claim from sounding more complete than it is. It also helps readers compare lane value without assuming the same behavior applies to creeps, heroes or every projectile context.

## Reactive utility: parry and defensive item timing

Deadlock auto parry and defensive-item assistance react to an incoming threat rather than continuously selecting a target. Their evaluation is mostly about boundaries and control: which threat class can trigger a response, whether the response is visible to the player, whether manual input can override it, and what happens when the situation changes. A broad “automatic defense” label hides those limits. Treat these features as reactive utility, then judge clarity and failure behavior—not the mere presence of a toggle or a promise that it will win an exchange.

## Hero automation: convenience versus predictability

Deadlock hero scripts package character-specific actions or ability sequences. Their appeal is convenience, but their useful quality is predictability. A reader should be able to tell which hero and situation a script targets, what sequence it intends, when it stops, and whether manual control remains available. That matters because a fixed routine can become a liability when range, cooldowns, targets or positioning change. More scripted actions do not automatically mean better play; transparent scope, easy interruption and understandable conditions make hero automation possible to evaluate.
