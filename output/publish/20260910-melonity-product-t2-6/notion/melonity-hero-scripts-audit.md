## Why hero count is a weak buying metric

“Supports many heroes” sounds useful until your comfort picks sit outside the maintained set. Even when a hero appears in the interface, support can mean very different things:

- one basic combo key;
- several configurable spell sequences;
- item logic tied to specific conditions;
- clone or secondary-unit control;
- defensive reactions;
- a large settings page with exclusions and thresholds.

These are not equivalent. A player who spams Meepo needs different evidence from a Pudge player. Arc Warden raises questions about Tempest Double control, while Invoker raises questions about spell selection and sequence logic. The listing publicly names Shadow Fiend, Invoker, Meepo, Arc Warden, Sky Mage and Pudge as examples. Treat that list as a starting point, not a permanent roster guarantee.

## Build a six-scenario hero checklist

Pick three heroes you genuinely use. For each hero, write two ordinary scenarios in plain language. Keep the checklist about observable behavior, not low-level implementation.

For example:

- **Invoker:** one common spell sequence and one interrupted sequence;
- **Arc Warden:** one normal hero action and one Tempest Double situation;
- **Pudge:** one targeting-assistance scenario and one case where the feature should remain inactive.

This gives support a concrete question to answer. “Does Invoker work?” invites a vague yes. “Is the currently listed Invoker module documented for these two situations?” is harder to dodge and easier to verify later.

## Separate hero logic from shared utility

A product can combine hero scripts with general-purpose settings. Do not credit a shared function to every hero as if it were unique hero depth. The gallery's General section, for example, shows inventory-related and unlocker-style options. Those may matter to a buyer, but they should not inflate the hero-script score.

A clean audit keeps four buckets:

1. hero-specific decisions and combos;
2. player-wide assistance;
3. information and map displays;
4. customization or inventory presentation.

That prevents a huge menu from turning into a fake “every hero has hundreds of functions” claim.

## Check control, not only automation

More automation is not automatically better. Good evaluation questions include:

- Can the user see which condition triggered an action?
- Can a feature be disabled without disturbing unrelated settings?
- Are thresholds and target scopes readable?
- Does the interface expose exclusions?
- Is there a clear difference between an active and inactive state?

The goal is not to find the most aggressive setup. It is to understand whether the configuration is legible enough that a user can predict what a toggle is supposed to change. Unclear automation creates bad decisions even before account-risk questions enter the picture.

## Common hero-coverage mistakes

- Counting every toggle as a separate hero script.
- Assuming a gallery hero is maintained forever.
- Treating one successful clip as proof across all targets and item states.
- Ignoring shared utility that can conflict with hero-specific controls.
- Enabling a large preset before understanding individual functions.
- Confusing Humanizer or AntiVAC labels with a no-ban guarantee.

Scenario depth is the number of clearly documented situations a hero module handles, including normal, interrupted and excluded states—not the raw number of toggles.

No third-party automation should be treated as risk-free. Product labels and screenshots cannot guarantee an account outcome.

### How many heroes should I audit before buying?

Three is a practical minimum: your main hero, a common backup and one mechanically different pick. Two scenarios per hero reveal more than a broad catalogue claim.
