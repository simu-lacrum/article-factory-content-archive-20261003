**Dota 2 cheat ban risk** cannot be reduced to “detected” or “undetected.” Enforcement can be delayed, a method can change after an update, and public user reports are incomplete. A period without visible bans is not proof that a product is safe.

The most reliable facts come from Valve and Steam. Vendor status pages can describe current availability, but they cannot guarantee future anti-cheat behavior.

## What VAC policy actually says

[Steam's official Valve Anti-Cheat explanation](https://help.steampowered.com/en/faqs/view/571A-97DA-70E9-FF74) says third-party modifications that give one player an advantage are classified as cheats. Steam also states that VAC bans are permanent and are not manually removed by support.

The policy does not publish signatures, timing or a list of currently detected products. Anyone claiming access to a complete public detection list is overstating the evidence.

## Why no recent reports does not mean no risk

Users may not report a ban, may attribute it to the wrong tool or may post long after detection. Sellers may remove negative reports. A small private user base can also produce too little public data to support a conclusion.

Delayed action makes short tests especially weak. A clean launch or several completed matches only proves that no visible enforcement occurred during that window.

## Valve has documented hidden-data detection

In [Valve's 40,000-account Dota enforcement report](https://www.dota2.com/newsentry/3677788723152833273), Valve explained that it added a data area ordinary clients would never read. Accounts using software that accessed the area were identified and permanently banned.

This example does not reveal the current system, but it disproves the idea that a working hidden-information feature is automatically invisible to the developer.

## Common safety claims and their limits

*   **“Private”** describes distribution, not immunity.
*   **“Paid”** describes price, not code quality.
*   **“Stream-proof”** describes capture behavior, not anti-cheat status.
*   **“External”** describes architecture broadly, not zero detection.
*   **“Updated today”** describes activity, not a verified safety horizon.

## Build a realistic account-risk model

Start with the value of the Steam account, inventory and match history. Add the chance of malware, credential loss, software downtime and permanent enforcement. Then ask whether the intended feature is worth the combined downside.

If any seller minimizes those consequences, treat that as a trust signal. ## How to read detection-status evidence

A status page can responsibly say that a specific build was tested on a specific date and that no issue was observed in that test. It cannot turn that limited observation into “safe forever.” Ask how the status is updated, whether unavailable periods remain visible and whether the seller distinguishes technical failure from confirmed enforcement.

User reports also need context. The product version, other installed tools, date of use and date of enforcement may all be unknown. One report cannot prove a universal cause, while silence cannot prove absence. Treat aggregated reports as warning signals, not a scientific detection rate.

The honest conclusion is conditional: a feature may be working now, but the account remains exposed to software, platform and future-update risk. That wording is less dramatic and more accurate.

Separate product uptime from detection status as well. A server can be online while a module is broken; a module can function while enforcement is delayed. A single green badge should not represent delivery, technical compatibility and account safety at the same time. Each claim needs its own date and meaning.

When several products are installed, attribution becomes even weaker. A user cannot reliably identify which component created a signal, so stacking tools makes public status reports less useful and increases the number of files that must be trusted.
