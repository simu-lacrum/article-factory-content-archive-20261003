## Examples of misleading map evidence

A marker appearing after a visible teleport effect does not prove access to the destination. A ward estimate based on where an enemy paused does not prove the exact ward slot. A last-seen trajectory does not prove the unit continued along that path. Product demonstrations should label these as observations, estimates or concealed-data claims.

False certainty changes player behavior. Rotating toward a stale icon, avoiding a ward that no longer exists or initiating on an illusion can produce worse decisions than ordinary map reading. Accuracy should therefore be assessed across failures, not only highlight clips.

Check whether the interface lets estimates expire and whether the user can distinguish them visually from confirmed positions. If every marker looks exact, the display hides uncertainty instead of helping the player interpret it.

A serious test should include negative cases: events that look suspicious but are explained by normal vision, sound, particles or prediction. Without those controls, every successful guess becomes “proof” and every miss disappears from the demonstration. That selection bias makes highlight reels a poor accuracy measure.

Patch notes can also invalidate the premise of a feature. When Valve changes which events reach the client, a previous alert may become delayed, incomplete or impossible. Require a new demonstration tied to the new build.

Keep the demonstration date beside every accuracy claim so an older result is not presented as current evidence after the client changes.
